# Jazz group-playing terms, sourced from published writing

Compiled 2026-08-10. Two sources, handled differently:

- **Jamey Aebersold, *Jazz Handbook*** ("The Red Book," free PDF, jazzbooks.com, © 2000/2010/2013/2017) — read in full, all 51 pages. Every term below is quoted or closely paraphrased from actual handbook text, with page numbers.
- **Paul F. Berliner, *Thinking in Jazz: The Infinite Art of Improvisation*** (University of Chicago Press, 1994) — this is the real book behind the title you remembered ("Jazz: The Infinite Art of Improvisation"). Full text is paywalled/library-only; I could not get past the table of contents. Terms below come from its published chapter titles (confirmed via UChicago Press and academic search), not body text — so this list is thin and chapter-level, not a true glossary.

Caveat on the Aebersold book: it's built almost entirely around **solo** improvisation pedagogy (scales, licks, ear training, transcribing). Real ensemble/group-interaction vocabulary in it is sparser than you'd expect from something with "Handbook" in the name — most of what's below leans solo-adjacent but is still real, sourced vocabulary, not invented.

## From Berliner's *Thinking in Jazz* (chapter titles + quotes found via a 1998 review)

Full text is still paywalled/library-only for me. But I found Jonathan Stock's review essay of the book (*British Journal of Ethnomusicology*, vol. 7, 1998, pp. 143–151), which quotes and page-cites Berliner directly. That gets us past chapter titles into a few real, quoted terms:

| Term | Berliner page (via Stock's citation) | Context |
|---|---|---|
| Ahead of the Beat | 151 | Drummer/bassist positioning relative to the pulse |
| Behind the Beat | 151 | Same passage |
| On the Beat | 151 | Same passage |
| "Elliptical figure" (of the beat) | 151 | Berliner's own image for how the beat is felt as a shape, not a point |
| Cutting Session | — | Referenced via the idiom "cut the mustard" — competitive sitting-in/proving-yourself sessions |
| Realist Tale | — | Stock's term for Berliner's narrative method (interviews + analysis + participant-observation woven together) — not a musician's term, but describes how the book itself works |

## From Ingrid Monson, *Saying Something: Jazz Improvisation and Interaction* (University of Chicago Press, 1996)

A direct peer to Berliner's book — same series (Chicago Studies in Ethnomusicology), same publisher, built specifically around ensemble interaction rather than solo pedagogy. Confirmed via UChicago Press and its Internet Archive listing (`archive.org/details/sayingsomethingj0000mons`), which is borrowable and full-text searchable if you want more from it directly.

| Chapter | Title | Terms |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Talking to Musicians | — |
| 2 | Grooving and Feeling | Groove, Feel/Feeling |
| 3 | Music, Language, and Cultural Styles: Improvisation as Conversation | Improvisation as Conversation |
| 4 | Intermusicality | Intermusicality (the book's signature coined term) |
| 5 | Interaction, Feeling, and Musical Analysis | Interaction |
| 6 | Ethnomusicology, Interaction, and Poststructuralism | — |
| 7 | Coda | — |

Monson's own framing (from the book description): rhythm-section players work "intermusically," producing a **groove** — she defines it specifically as "a polyrhythmic construct" that gives a performance its feel and pace. That's a real, citable definition, not paraphrase.

### Where to point your physical copy of *Thinking in Jazz*

If you're willing to open the book, these are the highest-yield spots based on what I could confirm exists:

1. **The index** (back of book) — fastest way to surface every named concept at once; photograph a few pages of it and I can build a real glossary from entries alone.
2. **p. 151** — the "ahead/behind/on the beat," "elliptical figure" passage (confirmed above).
3. **Chapters 11–15** (Part III, "Collective Aspects of Improvisation") — arranging, rhythm section conventions, "give and take," evaluating group performances, band life. This is almost certainly where documented musician slang (comping, trading fours, in the pocket, etc.) actually shows up in direct quotes from Berliner's 50+ interviews.
4. **Chapter 4, "Getting Your Vocabulary Straight"** — Berliner explicitly treats "vocabulary" as a term jazz musicians use about their own learning; worth a skim even though it's solo-focused.
